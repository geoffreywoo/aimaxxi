Before I divide two capacity figures, I check whose campus they describe.

An infrastructure number travels easily. Its project name, measurement boundary and tense often travel less well. Here are three real source statements. Keep the labels attached.

Which claim belongs here?

Can the 330 MW sentence establish utility service for Meta’s announced expansion?

No. Its paragraph names Hut 8’s River Bend campus. A shared utility, a shared state and a shared report page do not make it Meta’s Richland Parish project.

Does the older Meta-related approval report settle the expansion’s current power supply?

No. The project connection is relevant, but approval, a forecast date and delivered campus power are different claims. These three documents leave the later expansion’s service quantity and phase coverage unresolved.

Keep four labels beside the number.

  1. Project. Which campus, location and phase does the sentence actually name?
  2. Quantity. Copy the capacity noun and its boundary. Compute capacity, utility service, generating capacity and metered demand need their own definitions.
  3. Milestone. Is this an announcement, approval, contract, construction report, forecast or measured delivery? Preserve the source’s tense.
  4. Date. Separate publication day, reported event and expected completion. Check whether a later expansion changed the scope.

A number can be accurate and still answer the wrong question. Match the project first; then establish compatible boundaries and milestones before calculating.
